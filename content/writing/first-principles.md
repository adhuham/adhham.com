First principles thinking is often described as a genius move. In practice, it is closer to housekeeping.

It means refusing borrowed conclusions until you understand the parts they are made from.

## Start with the question behind the question

Before solving a problem, ask what assumption makes the problem seem inevitable.

- Why does this need to exist?
- What would break if it disappeared?
- Is the constraint real or inherited?

Many "hard problems" are hard because we inherited a frame that makes them hard.

## Decompose, then rebuild

Decomposition is not cynicism. It is respect for reality.

Break the idea into components. Identify which components are facts, which are conventions, and which are preferences dressed up as facts. Rebuild only what survives scrutiny.

## The cost of clarity

First principles work is slow at the beginning and fast at the end. It resists performative urgency.

But clarity compounds. A team that understands why a system exists will make better decisions than a team that only knows how to operate it.

That difference shows up everywhere: in code, in policy, in conversation, in a life.
