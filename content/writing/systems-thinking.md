Most problems we call "technical" are actually coordination problems wearing a costume.

When a system fails, we often reach for a local fix: patch the bug, add a dashboard, hire another engineer. These moves can help, but they rarely change the underlying structure that produced the failure in the first place.

## Seeing the whole

Systems thinking begins with a simple discipline: before optimizing a part, describe the whole.

- What are the inputs?
- What feedback loops exist?
- Where does information get lost?
- Who benefits when the system behaves this way?

A useful essay question is not "What should we build?" but "What behavior does this architecture reward?"

## Feedback over intention

Intentions are cheap. Feedback is expensive and honest.

The gap between what we say a system should do and what it actually does is where the real design work lives. Good thinkers spend less time defending plans and more time observing outcomes.

> A system is what it does, not what it claims to do.

## A practical habit

Once a week, pick one workflow you depend on and draw it on paper. Mark delays, handoffs, and invisible dependencies. You will almost always find a bottleneck that no single team owns.

That is not a failure of individuals. It is a property of the system.

And systems, unlike moods, can be redesigned.
